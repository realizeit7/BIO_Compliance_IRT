# METHODOLOGY.md — Methodological Decision Record

This file documents every substantive methodological choice made in this
project: what was decided, what the alternatives were, why the choice was made,
and what its limitations are. It is the intellectual companion to `CLAUDE.md`
(which records operational state). Every decision below actually happened —
nothing is reconstructed after the fact. Where a choice was tried and reversed,
the reversal is documented too, because the failed paths are part of the
methodology.

Format per decision: **Decision / Alternatives / Rationale / Limitations.**

---

## A. Item Generation

### A1. LLM-generated items with a mandatory Red Herring ("High-Subtlety" template)

**Decision.** Every hard synthetic item must contain (1) at least one
*compliant* process that looks suspicious (the Red Herring), (2) a genuinely
subtle violation (procedural lapse, signature-timing issue, or
system-to-system data-integrity gap — never a blatant breach), and (3) a gold
standard that explains why the Red Herring is *not* the violation.

**Alternatives.** Plain scenario generation without adversarial structure (the
original design, Experiments 1–5 in the git history); template-free generation
with difficulty controlled only by domain choice; human-authored items.

**Rationale.** The experiment history drove this. Exp 1 (lenient judging, plain
items) produced items every solver passed — no measurable difficulty. Exp 2
added counterintuitive hints and improved hard-item yield. The Red Herring
mandate generalizes that finding: difficulty in compliance judgment comes from
*discrimination between similar-looking cases*, not from obscure facts. An item
where the obvious suspicious thing is innocent and the real violation is quiet
forces exactly the discrimination that distinguishes expertise tiers.

**Limitations.** (1) Red Herring quality is never audited — items are generated
under the mandate but no automated check verifies a genuine Red Herring exists.
(2) The mandate makes hard items **violation-by-design**: of 987 QC'd
hard-domain items, only 16 have a COMPLIANT gold verdict (discovered
2026-06-11 while building the expert-review sample). A test where ~97% of
items have the same binary verdict is exploitable by a constant-answer
strategy and limits what expert verdict-agreement can measure. Any bank
regeneration must mandate ~50% genuinely-compliant scenarios.

### A2. Dual-track grounding: synthetic items + real FDA warning letters

**Decision.** Run a parallel ingestion track (`fda_importer.py`) that converts
actual FDA warning letters into calibration items tagged `source_type="real"`,
calibrated identically to synthetic items.

**Alternatives.** Synthetic-only (cheaper, unlimited supply); real-only (too
few documents, no difficulty control); human-authored exam items (no access to
SMEs at construction time).

**Rationale.** A synthetic-only bank is a fully closed loop: the same model
family writes, solves, and judges the items, so "difficulty" might measure
nothing outside that loop. Real enforcement letters are generated by an
external process (FDA inspections), so the difficulty distribution of
real-sourced items is an external anchor. The comparison produced a real
finding: real items skew harder than synthetic (mean b gap +1.14 at n=54
early on; +0.49 on the full Phase 1 calibration with n=105 real items), and
no real item lands in the easy band — FDA enforcement does not produce easy
questions.

**Limitations.** The letters are sourced externally, but the *items* are still
LLM-written: an LLM reads the letter and drafts the context, question, and
gold standard. The grounding is partial — provenance of the violation is real,
phrasing and answer key are not. The b-gap has no significance test yet
(Mann-Whitney U named as a gap in CLAUDE.md). Promotional-review letters
(OPDP) use a different URL structure and are absent from the real track —
domain coverage is uneven.

## B. Examinee Population

### B1. Synthetic examinee populations: 15 profiles, then a 45-cell grid

**Decision.** Difficulty is estimated from the pass rates of *synthetic
examinees* — first 15 solver profiles (5 expertise tiers × 3 temperatures) in
the legacy pipeline, then a 45-cell grid (3 base models × 5 temperatures × 3
system-prompt strictness levels) in the Phase 1 calibration.

**Alternatives.** The original 2-profile design (Vanilla vs Augmented); human
test-takers (the psychometric gold standard, typically 200–1000+ examinees);
more model families (limited by what Groq hosts).

**Rationale.** Two profiles give a 3-point pass-rate scale — useless for
calibration. Widening to 15 profiles, and then to a 45-cell grid spanning
*three different model families*, increases the score variance available for
item calibration and breaks single-model homogeneity. Temperature and prompt
strictness are used as cheap, controllable ability-proxies. The grid is fully
crossed, so every examinee attempts every item — no missing-data machinery
needed.

**Limitations.** This is the project's deepest methodological compromise.
Forty-five virtual examinees built from 3 base models are not independent
test-takers: cells sharing a base model are highly correlated, so the
effective sample size is far below 45. IRT calibration assumes independent
examinees; the b estimates are best read as "difficulty for this population of
machine solvers," not population-general item difficulty. This is why the
human expert review track (Section G) exists.

### B2. Population-logit difficulty formula in the legacy pipeline

**Decision.** Legacy-pipeline item difficulty is computed directly from the
empirical pass rate P over the 15 profiles: `b = ln((1−P)/P)`, with P clipped
to [0.01, 0.99]. Retention thresholds: P < 0.05 → discarded too-hard,
P > 0.95 → retained-easy, otherwise retained (widened 2026-04-14 from
0.10/0.90 to keep near-extreme items).

**Alternatives.** The original design sampled b from Uniform difficulty bands
(essentially made-up numbers); a full IRT fit per batch (overkill for a
streaming pipeline ingesting a few items at a time).

**Rationale.** Under the Rasch model, if the examinee population is centered
at θ=0, then P(pass) = 1/(1+e^b), which inverts to the logit formula. It is
the simplest estimator consistent with the measurement model, computable
per-item with no joint fit, which suits incremental bank building.

**Limitations.** The centering assumption (mean profile ability = 0) is
untested. If the 15 profiles lean expert, every b is biased downward. The
clipping bounds b to ±4.6 regardless of evidence. Legacy DB rows from earlier
eras carry band-sampled b (identifiable by `a ≠ 1.0`) and must never be
averaged with logit-b rows — a real foot-gun documented in CLAUDE.md.

## C. Measurement Model

### C1. Rasch (1PL) instead of 2PL

**Decision.** All official calibration (Phase 1, Phase 2, Phase 3, autoevolve
fitness) uses the Rasch model: fixed discrimination a=1, item difficulty b
estimated by marginal maximum likelihood (`girth.rasch_mml`).

**Alternatives.** 2PL was not just considered — it was the *active path* on
the morning of 2026-05-10 (`evaluator/twopl.py` + `qc.py`, using
`girth.twopl_jml` with an a ≤ 0.3 drop rule) before being demoted the same
day. 3PL was never attempted (guessing parameter is meaningless for free-text
responses).

**Rationale.** Empirical, not aesthetic. 2PL via marginal MLE pinned every
item's discrimination at a=5.0 — girth's upper bound — a classic sign the
likelihood is flat in *a* for this examinee count. 2PL via joint MLE produced
a spread, but JML is statistically inconsistent as the item count grows with
examinee count fixed, which is exactly this design (1,823 items, 45
examinees). Rasch is identifiable in that regime, and its *specific
objectivity* property (examinee comparisons do not depend on which item
subset is used) is precisely what Phase 3's Δθ comparisons and autoevolve's
40-item fitness samples rely on. The 2PL machinery was kept dormant, not
deleted, for when the examinee count grows.

**Limitations.** Equal discrimination is an assumption, and the Phase 2 QC
(point-biserial spread 0.004–0.757) shows items in fact discriminate
unevenly. Misfit that 2PL would absorb into *a* instead lands in Rasch's fit
statistics and the QC drop list — acceptable, but it means ~30% of items were
dropped rather than down-weighted.

### C2. Anchoring the scale to a designated baseline examinee at θ = 0

**Decision.** After each Rasch fit, every b is shifted so that one
config-designated baseline examinee (llama-3.1-8b-instant, t=0.1, no system
prompt) has MLE θ exactly 0.

**Alternatives.** Mean-zero centering over all examinees (the conventional
identification constraint); fixing the item-difficulty mean instead.

**Rationale.** Logit scales have an arbitrary origin; some constraint is
mandatory. Anchoring to a *named, reproducible* examinee makes every θ in the
project directly interpretable: "+1.78 means 1.78 logits above a bare 8B model
at low temperature." Mean-centering would re-define the origin every time the
examinee grid changes; the baseline examinee is stable across phases.

**Limitations.** The anchor is only as stable as the baseline's own estimate.
Two real incidents demonstrate the fragility: (1) fixing the gpt-oss-20b
empty-response bug legitimately moved the baseline raw θ from −0.513 to
−0.626, shifting every b; (2) restricting from the full 1,823-item set to the
1,284-item frozen bank shifts the baseline's θ by ≈ −0.72, so Phase 3 must
re-anchor on the subset before absolute θ values are honest. Within-model Δθ
comparisons are invariant to these shifts; absolute θ values are not, and
must always state their anchor.

### C3. Parameter uncertainty: analytic SE now, Bayesian posterior SD later

**Decision.** Report the delta-method standard error
`SE(b) = 1/√(N·P(1−P))` (N=45, P recovered from b) for every frozen-bank
item, while keeping a py-irt (Pyro variational) 1PL fit
(`evaluator/pyirt_fit.py`) as the intended final uncertainty path — verified
on synthetic data (recovery r ≈ 0.93) but blocked on regenerating the raw
response log.

**Alternatives.** Reporting point estimates only (girth returns no standard
errors — the de facto default); bootstrap over examinees (45 correlated cells
make resampling units ambiguous).

**Rationale.** "IRT-calibrated" without uncertainty is not a defensible claim;
the honest-assessment section of CLAUDE.md flags exactly this. The analytic SE
is the standard one-item logit SE, costs zero API calls, and was wired into
the paper figures. The Bayesian path gives genuinely per-item posterior SDs —
notably *wider* than the analytic SEs (vague priors, 45 examinees), which is
treated as the more honest number, not a defect.

**Limitations.** The analytic SE assumes 45 *independent* Bernoulli trials per
item; with correlated examinee cells it understates true uncertainty,
possibly substantially. The py-irt fit cannot run until the gitignored Phase 1
response log (~164k Groq calls) is regenerated.

## D. Judging

### D1. LLM-as-judge with two strictness modes, assigned by item type

**Decision.** A binary PASS/FAIL LLM judge with two modes: **strict**
(requires the correct conclusion AND a section-level citation, e.g.
"21 CFR 11.10(e)") for hard and real items; **lenient** (correct conclusion +
sound reasoning, no citation requirement) for easy items. Judge errors map to
FAIL.

**Alternatives.** Single lenient judge (Exp 1: every solver passed everything;
no item difficulty signal). Single strict judge for all items (would make easy
items unpassable for the layperson profiles — they never cite sections — so
the low end of the θ scale would have no items to anchor it, and θ MLE for
weak profiles would not converge). Graded (polytomous) scoring; human grading.

**Rationale.** The two-mode design emerged from failures of both single-mode
options (Exp 1 → Exp 3/4 in the experiment history). The citation requirement
is what makes hard items hard for fluent-but-ungrounded solvers; waiving it
on easy items is what lets weak profiles pass anything at all. Binary scoring
keeps the Rasch model applicable.

**Limitations.** The judge is the same model family as the solvers — grader
and graded share failure modes, the central closed-loop critique. Judge error
rate is non-zero (~4.3% in the Phase 3a lenient re-grade) and silently counts
as solver failure. No human grading of any subset has occurred yet.

### D2. Dual-judge decomposition of intervention effects (Phase 3a/3b)

**Decision.** Measure every intervention's Δθ twice — once under the strict
judge and once under a lenient re-grade of the *same saved responses* — and
report the difference as the **format-unlock** component (the part of the
gain that is just learning to cite) versus the **reasoning gain** (the part
that survives when citations are not required).

**Alternatives.** Report a single strict-judge Δθ (what nearly every
prompt-engineering result in the literature reports); manual error analysis
of response samples.

**Rationale.** A "system prompt improves compliance performance by 5.4
logits" headline is ambiguous between teaching the model to *format* and
teaching it to *reason*. Re-grading existing responses leniently costs only
judge calls (no new solver calls) and cleanly splits the two. The result
reframed the headline: llama-3.3-70b's +5.41 strict-judge swing decomposes
into ≈+2.5 reasoning and ≈+2.9 format; for gpt-oss-20b and llama-3.1-8b the
lenient Δθ is ≈0 — their entire "prompt effect" is citation formatting.
Phase 3b then showed the same pattern for *architectural* interventions
(retrieval, self-critique, step decomposition): gains under lenient scoring,
losses under strict. This decomposition is arguably the most defensible
empirical contribution of the project.

**Limitations.** "Lenient" is still an LLM judge, with its own error rate.
The decomposition is additive by construction; interactions between format
and reasoning are not modeled. Only one temperature (t=0.4) was tested.

## E. Bank Quality Control (Phase 2)

### E1. Classical QC signals with asymmetric fit bounds, instead of 2PL-based screening

**Decision.** Freeze the bank by dropping: (1) zero-variance items (P=0 or
P=1); (2) items with corrected point-biserial ≤ 0 (inverted items — stronger
examinees fail more); (3) infit > 1.5 or outfit > 2.0, *upper bounds only*,
with outfit's bound looser than infit's. Result: 1,823 → 1,284 items (70.4%
retention).

**Alternatives.** The 2PL a ≤ 0.3 screen (the active path that morning —
demoted with the 2PL fit itself, see C1); Linacre's standard symmetric fit
window [0.5, 1.5]; no QC at all (rely on the generation-time pass-rate
filter).

**Rationale.** Each classical signal replaces a 2PL diagnostic with a
model-light equivalent: negative point-biserial catches exactly the
broken-ground-truth items that negative discrimination would have caught.
The asymmetric bounds are a deliberate small-sample adaptation: Linacre's
symmetric window is calibrated for thousands of examinees, where a too-low
mean-square indicates redundancy. On 45 cells, a low mean-square item is a
clean separator — a feature, not a defect — so only the noisy (high) side is
penalized. Outfit gets the looser bound (2.0 vs 1.5) because it is
unweighted, and on a small grid a single anomalous response at extreme θ
blows it up disproportionately.

**Limitations.** The thresholds are judgment calls, not derived from a
simulation study. 539 dropped items include 308 zero-variance items that
might be informative for a stronger or weaker examinee population than this
grid. Fit statistics were computed with MLE θ — fine here because all 45
examinees have finite θ, but fragile in general.

## F. Intervention Measurement and Optimization

### F1. The frozen bank's Δθ as the single effect-size ruler

**Decision.** After Phase 2, the 1,284-item frozen bank is treated as a fixed
measurement instrument. Every subsequent question — does prompt strictness
help (3a)? do retrieval/critic/decomposition architectures help (3b)? did an
autoevolve mutation help? — is answered as a Δθ on this same bank. The bank
is never modified by downstream consumers.

**Alternatives.** Raw pass-rate deltas per experiment (the field's default);
re-calibrating items per experiment (lets the ruler drift with the thing
being measured); separate ad-hoc test sets per phase.

**Rationale.** Pass-rate deltas depend on which items were sampled; under
Rasch's specific objectivity, θ comparisons do not (up to noise). Putting
prompt effects, architecture effects, and evolved-pipeline gains on one logit
scale makes them directly comparable — e.g., autoevolve's adopted mutations
(+0.5 to +1.3 per adoption) can be honestly compared against the Phase 3a
prompt-strictness swing (+5.4) measured on the same ruler. Freezing also
prevents the subtle circularity of optimizing against a moving target.

**Limitations.** Specific objectivity holds exactly only if the Rasch model
holds exactly; with ~30% of items QC-dropped, the surviving bank fits
reasonably but not perfectly. The bank's b values inherit every upstream
limitation (closed loop, correlated examinees, anchor shifts).

### F2. autoevolve: IRT ability as a fitness function for pipeline evolution

**Decision.** An evolutionary loop where an LLM proposer mutates one element
of a 3-step solver pipeline (triage → analysis → verdict) per iteration —
one prompt, template, model, or token budget — and the mutation is adopted
iff the candidate's MLE θ on a 40-item band-stratified sample of the frozen
bank exceeds the champion's by ≥ 0.5 logits (≈1 SE). Rejected
(change-type, step, model) triples are blacklisted within a run.

**Alternatives.** Raw pass rate as fitness (ignores that beating hard items
should count for more than beating easy ones); DSPy / OPRO / APE-style prompt
optimizers (not run — named explicitly as the missing baseline);
multi-change mutations (confound attribution); human-designed ablations only
(Phase 3b is exactly this, and autoevolve is its automated complement).

**Rationale.** θ is a difficulty-weighted score with a known scale and an
approximate SE, which gives a principled adoption threshold: min-delta 0.5
≈ 1 SE of θ on 40 items, so adoptions are unlikely to be pure noise.
Single-change mutation keeps every adoption causally attributable. The
empirical trajectory: champion θ went from ≈ −0.04 to +1.78 over 28
frozen-bank iterations with 4 adoptions (14% adoption rate); the wins were
concentrated in Step 1 (analysis) model and template changes, while Step 0
(triage) edits and exotic model swaps (qwen-qwq, deepseek-r1) consistently
failed — itself an interpretable finding about where the pipeline's headroom
is.

**Limitations.** θ on 40 items is noisy (SE ≈ 0.5), so the threshold trades
false adoptions against missed real gains — three near-misses at Δθ ≈ +0.43
to +0.45 were rejected and may have been real. Champion θ measured in
different iterations fluctuates by ±0.5+ from sampling alone. No comparison
against DSPy/OPRO exists, so "is IRT-fitness better than standard prompt
optimization?" is open — this is the single most important gap for the
autoevolve write-up. The proposer is a small model whose hypotheses are
shallow; many mutations are paraphrases of each other.

## G. Human Validation (in progress)

### G1. 50-item expert review with a two-step blind protocol

**Decision.** Export a stratified 50-item sample (10 per hard domain; QC
filter pb ≥ 0.3, infit ≤ 1.3, outfit ≤ 1.5; difficulty restricted to the
P20–P80 band b ∈ [0.83, 2.43]; COMPLIANT-verdict items exempt from the band
because only 16 exist; seed=42, fully reproducible). Reviewers follow a
two-step protocol: (1) give their own VIOLATION/COMPLIANT verdict from
context + question alone, *then* (2) read the LLM gold standard and grade it
correct/incorrect. The answer key (b values, source types, derived verdicts)
is withheld from reviewers.

**Alternatives.** Grade-the-gold-only protocol (faster, but yields no
independent human verdicts, hence no inter-rater agreement and no human
difficulty anchor); random sampling without QC/band filters (wastes expert
hours on items already known to be defective or degenerate); a full-bank
review (~100× the cost).

**Rationale.** The two-step design extracts two measurements from one pass:
expert-vs-expert agreement (is the task well-posed?) and expert-vs-LLM
agreement (what is the gold-standard error rate?). Step 1 before Step 2
prevents anchoring on the LLM's answer. The middle difficulty band targets
the items where calibration matters most and where expert judgment is most
informative — extreme items are either trivial or broken.

**Limitations.** The verdict balance is 38 VIOLATION / 12 COMPLIANT — the
structural ceiling given A1's violation-by-design generation, and far from
the ideal 25/25; a reviewer who answers VIOLATION on everything scores 76%,
which caps how much verdict agreement alone can tell us (the
gold-standard-grading step is less affected). Labels are not yet collected;
nothing downstream is validated until they are.

## H. Cross-Cutting Limitations

Stated once, applying to everything above:

1. **The loop is mostly closed.** One model family (plus two others from the
   same open-weight ecosystem, all served by one inference vendor) generates
   the items, solves them, and judges the solutions. The FDA real-world track
   (A2) and the expert review (G1) are the only points where external signal
   enters, and the expert labels are still pending.
2. **The examinee population is synthetic and small.** Everything calibrated
   here is calibrated *for machine solvers*. No claim about human difficulty
   ordering is supported until the expert anchor exists.
3. **Single-vendor inference.** All measurements ran on Groq-hosted models.
   Provider-side changes (quantization, serving stack) are an uncontrolled
   variable across runs — partially mitigated by resumable logs that pin each
   response, but never fully.
4. **No preregistration; thresholds are judgment calls.** Retention bounds
   (0.05/0.95), QC bounds (pb 0, infit 1.5, outfit 2.0), the autoevolve
   adoption delta (0.5), and the expert-sample band (P20–P80) were all chosen
   by reasoning, not by simulation or pilot data. Each is documented with its
   rationale above, which is the honest substitute available.

## I. Judge Calibration Audit

### I1. External metric comparison: DeepEval G-Eval and RAGAS on Phase 3b zero-shot responses

**Decision.** Compare the project's IRT binary judge against four continuous external
metrics — DeepEval G-Eval (two rubric variants) and RAGAS (faithfulness and
answer_correctness) — on 400 Phase 3b zero-shot responses (100 frozen-bank items × 4
zero-shot cells). All external metrics routed through Groq `llama-3.3-70b-versatile` to
hold grader capability constant; no new solver calls (reuses existing
`logs/arena_runs/phase3b_variants/responses.jsonl`). Analyses: response-level AUC vs IRT
verdict (Mann-Whitney U/n₁n₂), per-cell Spearman ρ vs Rasch θ, difficulty-confounded
leniency slope (metric score regressed on b within PASS / within FAIL groups), and top-40
disagreement cases for qualitative inspection.

**Alternatives.** (a) GPT-4o or Claude as grader — rejected because no OpenAI/Anthropic API key
exists in this project, and mixing grader families would conflate framework differences with
capability differences. (b) Skip G-Eval/RAGAS — rejected because the paper's related-work
argument needs empirical evidence that generic metrics miss what IRT captures. (c) Use new
solver calls — unnecessary given Phase 3b responses already cover the same items and cells.

**Rationale.** Holding grader model constant (Groq llama-3.3-70b) isolates the *framework*
difference (rubric design, aggregation logic, score scale) rather than capability. The two
G-Eval rubrics were written to match the project's strict judge (citation-requiring) and
lenient judge (conclusion-only) respectively, so the AUC comparison is a direct rubric-equivalence
test. RAGAS `faithfulness` was included *as a contrast*: it scores groundedness to context, not
correctness — predicting near-chance AUC is the hypothesis, not a failure mode. Using
`AnswerCorrectness(weights=[1.0, 0.0])` disables the embedding component entirely, making the
metric LLM-only and reproducible without sentence-transformers.

**Results (2026-06-12, n=400 responses, 93/1600 scoring errors excluded):**

| metric | AUC vs IRT | r_pb | rho_θ | slope\|PASS | slope\|FAIL |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| geval_strict | **0.838** | 0.570 | 1.000 | +0.015 | −0.019 |
| geval_lenient | 0.731 | 0.381 | 1.000 | −0.015 | −0.052 |
| ragas_faithfulness | 0.530 | 0.057 | −0.800 | −0.073 | −0.056 |
| ragas_answer_correctness | 0.693 | 0.315 | 0.800 | −0.003 | −0.017 |

`geval_strict` AUC=0.838 confirms that a citation-requiring rubric is a good proxy for the
IRT judge. `ragas_faithfulness` AUC≈0.530 (near chance) confirms the contrast hypothesis:
groundedness is a different construct from compliance correctness. Difficulty slopes near zero
conditional on verdict indicate none of the external metrics strongly confound item difficulty
with response quality — though they also do not separate b from θ by design the way IRT does.
`rho_θ=1.000` for G-Eval (n=4 cells) is directionally consistent with IRT's cell ordering
but statistically uninformative at n=4.

**Limitations.** (a) Same-family grader means we cannot separate "G-Eval agrees with IRT"
from "llama-3.3-70b agrees with itself" — the AUC is a rubric-design test, not a
model-independence test. (b) n=4 cells makes all Spearman ρ values uninterpretable as
significance tests. (c) 5.8% scoring errors (connection timeouts) are handled by the
resumable skip-if-done pattern but reduce effective n for RAGAS metrics (363 and 344
respectively). (d) The audit covers zero-shot responses only; agent-variant responses may
show different patterns (retrieval, critic, and step-decomp agents all boost G-Eval under
lenient judging but drop under strict judging — same direction as the Phase 3a finding).
