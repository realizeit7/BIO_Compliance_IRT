\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Benchmarks for language models.}
General-purpose benchmarks such as MMLU \citep{hendrycks2021mmlu},
HellaSwag \citep{zellers2019hellaswag}, and BIG-Bench
\citep{srivastava2023bigbench} report aggregate accuracy over large,
static item sets. They are useful for coarse ranking, but they do not
provide per-item difficulty parameters, standard errors, or a latent
scale on which to compare interventions. Expert-domain benchmarks narrow
the topic while keeping the same format. MedQA \citep{jin2021medqa} and
PubMedQA \citep{jin2019pubmedqa} draw on medical licensing exams and on
the biomedical literature, and MultiMedQA \citep{singhal2023clinical}
assembles several such sets with clinician-graded answers. These are
closer to the setting I care about, because a wrong answer has real
consequences. However, their items were written and calibrated for
\emph{human} examinees, and the reported outcome is still accuracy rather
than a position on a latent scale. As far as I know, no comparable
instrument exists for pharmaceutical regulatory compliance, where the
governing text is a body of enforceable regulation rather than a clinical
literature.

\paragraph{IRT for evaluating machines.}
A growing line of work applies item response theory to model evaluation,
using IRT to identify informative items, to weight benchmark scores, and
to compare systems on a latent ability scale
\citep{lalor2016building,vania2021comparing,rodriguez2021evaluation,maiapolo2024tinybenchmarks}.
\citet{lalor2016building} first fit IRT to NLP test items in order to
build an evaluation scale. \citet{vania2021comparing} use IRT to compare
existing test sets. \citet{rodriguez2021evaluation} show that leaderboard
examples differ sharply in how informative they are, and argue for
weighting them accordingly. \citet{maiapolo2024tinybenchmarks} use the
same structure to compress LLM benchmarks to a small fraction of their
items. The common thread is that IRT is applied after the fact to item
sets that already exist, and the analysis then selects or reweights
within those sets.

The work in this paper runs in the opposite direction. The items are
generated \emph{for} machine examinees in a domain with no human
calibration pool to inherit from, the difficulty mandate is built into
generation rather than discovered afterward, and the resulting scale is
used prospectively as an optimization target for comparing prompts and
agent architectures (Sections~\ref{sec:deltatheta}
and~\ref{sec:phase3b}). The cost of working this way is that I cannot
borrow an existing calibration as ground truth. That is why judge
auditing (Section~\ref{sec:judgeaudit}) and expert review
(Section~\ref{sec:expertreview}) carry more weight here than they would
if I were reusing a human-calibrated instrument.

\paragraph{LLMs as judges.}
Grading free-text compliance answers at scale requires an automated
judge, and the standard approach is to use another language model
\citep{zheng2023judge}. That work also documents the failure modes of the
approach, including position, verbosity, and self-enhancement biases. My
judge is subject to the same concerns, and Section~\ref{sec:judgeaudit}
measures how far its verdicts agree with two independently written
evaluation frameworks \citep{liu2023geval,es2024ragas}.
