\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{LLM benchmarks.}
General-purpose benchmarks such as MMLU \citep{hendrycks2021mmlu},
HellaSwag \citep{zellers2019hellaswag}, and BIG-Bench
\citep{srivastava2023bigbench} report aggregate accuracy over large,
static item sets. They are invaluable for coarse ranking but do not
provide per-item difficulty parameters, standard errors, or a latent
scale on which to compare interventions. Expert-domain benchmarks narrow
the topic but keep the format: MedQA \citep{jin2021medqa} and PubMedQA
\citep{jin2019pubmedqa} draw on medical licensing exams and biomedical
literature, and MultiMedQA \citep{singhal2023clinical} assembles several
such sets with clinician-graded answers. These are closer to our setting
--- a high-stakes domain where a wrong answer has consequences --- but
they inherit items written and calibrated for \emph{human} examinees,
and they still report accuracy rather than a latent scale. To our
knowledge no comparable instrument exists for pharmaceutical regulatory
compliance, where the governing text is a body of enforceable regulation
rather than a clinical literature.

\paragraph{IRT for evaluating machines.}
A growing line of work applies item-response theory to model evaluation,
using IRT to identify informative items, to weight benchmark scores, and
to compare systems on a latent ability scale
\citep{lalor2016building,vania2021comparing,rodriguez2021evaluation,maiapolo2024tinybenchmarks}.
\citet{lalor2016building} first fit IRT to NLP test items to build an
evaluation scale; \citet{vania2021comparing} use IRT to compare existing
test sets; \citet{rodriguez2021evaluation} show that leaderboard examples
differ sharply in informativeness and argue for weighting them
accordingly; and \citet{maiapolo2024tinybenchmarks} exploit the same
structure to compress LLM benchmarks to a fraction of their items. The
common thread is that IRT is applied \emph{post hoc} to item sets that
already exist --- typically assembled for human examinees or scraped for
another purpose --- and the analysis selects or reweights within them.

Our work inverts that order. The items are generated \emph{for} machine
examinees in a domain with no human calibration pool to inherit from, the
difficulty mandate is built into generation rather than discovered after
the fact, and the resulting scale is used prospectively --- as an
optimization target for comparing prompts and agent architectures
(Sections~\ref{sec:deltatheta} and~\ref{sec:phase3b}) --- rather than
retrospectively to prune a benchmark. The cost of that inversion is that
we cannot borrow an existing calibration as ground truth, which is why
judge auditing (Section~\ref{sec:judgeaudit}) and expert review
(Section~\ref{sec:expertreview}) carry more weight here than they would
in a reuse setting.

\paragraph{IRT in educational measurement.}
The Rasch (1PL) model \citep{rasch1960} and the broader IRT family
\citep{lord1980} are the foundation of modern high-stakes testing.
We adopt Rasch for its specific objectivity---ability differences are
invariant to which items are used---which is precisely the property we
need when comparing $\theta$ across prompt interventions
(Section~\ref{sec:deltatheta}). We use the \texttt{girth} library
\citep{girth} for marginal-maximum-likelihood estimation and
\texttt{py-irt} \citep{lalor2019pyirt} for posterior uncertainty.

\paragraph{LLM-as-judge.}
Using a strong LLM to grade open-ended responses is now common practice
\citep{zheng2023judge}. We adopt it out of necessity---there is no human
grading pool---and mitigate its known biases with a domain-specific
rubric and a strict/lenient dual-judge design
(Section~\ref{sec:itemgen}). We treat judge error as a measured quantity
rather than assuming it away (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
