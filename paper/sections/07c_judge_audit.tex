\section{Auditing the Judge Against Independent Frameworks}
\label{sec:judgeaudit}

Every result so far rests on a single measurement device: an LLM judge
that maps a free-text response to a binary verdict. If that judge is
idiosyncratic, the entire difficulty scale inherits its idiosyncrasy.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} concedes that the loop is closed; this
section asks how far the closure actually bites, by scoring the same
responses with two evaluation frameworks we did not write.

\subsection{Design}
We sampled $400$ responses from the Phase~3b log --- $100$ frozen-bank
items $\times$ $4$ zero-shot cells (two models $\times$ two strictness
levels) --- and re-scored each with four continuous metrics from two
external libraries. No new solver calls were required; the audit reuses
responses already collected.

\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{G-Eval strict} \citep{liu2023geval}: a chain-of-thought
        rubric that, like our judge, requires a section-level regulatory
        citation.
  \item \textbf{G-Eval lenient}: the same rubric with the citation
        requirement removed, scoring conclusion and reasoning only.
  \item \textbf{RAGAS faithfulness} \citep{es2024ragas}: groundedness of
        the response in the supplied scenario context.
  \item \textbf{RAGAS answer correctness}: agreement with the gold
        standard (factual component only; no embedding similarity term).
\end{itemize}

All four run on the same base model as our judge
(\texttt{llama-3.3-70b-versatile}). This is deliberate: holding the
grader's raw capability fixed isolates the effect of the \emph{rubric}
from the effect of the \emph{model}. It also bounds the claim --- what
follows is a test of rubric convergence, not an independent oracle
(Section~\ref{sec:judgeaudit-limits}).

We treat the IRT judge's binary verdict as the reference and ask how well
each continuous metric separates \textsc{pass} from \textsc{fail},
reporting the area under the ROC curve (AUC) and the point-biserial
correlation. Of $1{,}600$ scoring calls, $93$ ($5.8\%$) failed on
transient errors and are excluded pairwise; the RAGAS metrics therefore
have smaller $n$.

\subsection{Results}

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{External metrics versus the IRT judge's binary verdict on $400$
         Phase~3b zero-shot responses. AUC is the probability that a
         randomly chosen \textsc{pass} response scores above a randomly
         chosen \textsc{fail} response; $0.5$ is chance.}
\label{tab:judgeaudit}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Metric & $n$ & AUC vs.\ IRT verdict & $r_{pb}$ \\
\midrule
G-Eval strict (citation rubric)   & 400 & \textbf{0.838} & $+0.570$ \\
G-Eval lenient (conclusion only)  & 400 & 0.731 & $+0.381$ \\
RAGAS answer correctness          & 344 & 0.693 & $+0.315$ \\
RAGAS faithfulness (groundedness) & 363 & 0.530 & $+0.057$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:judgeaudit} shows a clean ordering, and it is the
ordering the design predicts.

\paragraph{Convergent validity.}
The citation-requiring G-Eval rubric tracks our judge most closely
($\mathrm{AUC} = 0.838$). Two rubrics written independently, sharing only
the stipulation that a compliance answer must cite the governing
provision, agree on which responses pass roughly five times out of six.
Removing the citation clause from that same rubric drops agreement to
$0.731$ --- direct evidence that the citation requirement, not some
incidental stylistic preference, is what our judge is enforcing.

\paragraph{Discriminant validity.}
RAGAS faithfulness sits essentially at chance ($\mathrm{AUC} = 0.530$,
$r_{pb} = +0.06$). This is the most informative number in the table. A
response can be perfectly grounded in the scenario it was given ---
inventing no facts, contradicting nothing --- and still reach the wrong
compliance conclusion. Groundedness and correctness are different
constructs, and a metric that measures the former carries almost no
information about the latter. That a widely used RAG metric fails to
recover compliance correctness is a caution about metric selection in
regulated domains, not a defect of RAGAS: it is measuring what it claims
to measure.

\paragraph{Difficulty is not confounded with quality.}
A leniency artifact would show up as external scores drifting with item
difficulty \emph{within} a verdict class. Regressing each metric's score
on $b$ separately within \textsc{pass} and \textsc{fail} responses gives
slopes near zero for G-Eval strict ($+0.015$, $p = 0.18$ within
\textsc{pass}; $-0.019$, $p = 0.03$ within \textsc{fail}). The remaining
metrics show small but significant negative slopes within \textsc{fail}
(steepest: RAGAS faithfulness, $-0.073$, $p < 10^{-7}$), i.e.\ failing
responses to harder items are scored slightly lower still. The IRT
separation of $b$ from response quality is therefore not perfectly
reproduced by the external tools, but neither is it strongly violated.

\paragraph{Cell-level ordering.}
Averaging each metric within the four examinee cells and ranking against
the Rasch $\theta$ for those cells, both G-Eval variants reproduce the
$\theta$ ordering exactly (Spearman $\rho = 1.00$) and RAGAS faithfulness
inverts it ($\rho = -0.80$). With only four cells this is descriptive,
not inferential --- any monotone metric has a one-in-twelve chance of
perfect agreement --- and we report it as a consistency check rather than
evidence.

\subsection{What the audit does and does not establish}
\label{sec:judgeaudit-limits}
The audit rules out one specific failure mode: that our judge's verdicts
are arbitrary, or that they encode a preference unrelated to the stated
rubric. An independently authored citation-aware rubric recovers those
verdicts at $\mathrm{AUC} = 0.838$, and a groundedness metric ---
correctly --- does not.

It does not establish that the verdicts are \emph{right}. All four
metrics run on the same base model, and all four consume the same
LLM-authored gold standards. A shared blind spot in the underlying model
family, or an error in a gold standard, is invisible to every metric in
Table~\ref{tab:judgeaudit}. Convergence among rubrics that share a
substrate is weaker evidence than convergence across substrates, and
weaker still than agreement with a human expert. That is the gap
Section~\ref{sec:expertreview} is designed to close.

To make the residual disagreement inspectable rather than merely
acknowledged, we release the $40$ strongest conflicts --- responses
scoring high on G-Eval strict but judged \textsc{fail}, and the converse
--- in \texttt{judge\_audit\_disagreements.jsonl}.
