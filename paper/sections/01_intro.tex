\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

% Motivation: why calibrated difficulty (not accuracy %) matters in GxP.
Large language models are increasingly proposed as assistants in
GxP-regulated environments---drug manufacturing, clinical trials, and
regulatory submissions---where an incorrect compliance determination
carries legal and patient-safety consequences. Yet the dominant way we
measure LLM competence, a single accuracy percentage on a fixed test set,
is poorly suited to this setting. Accuracy conflates easy and hard items,
gives no per-item difficulty, and cannot place a new system on a stable
scale for comparison across time or across interventions.

% Gap.
Educational measurement solved an analogous problem decades ago with
\emph{item-response theory} (IRT), which separates item difficulty
($b$) from examinee ability ($\theta$) on a common latent scale
\citep{lord1980,rasch1960}. IRT is standard for human high-stakes
testing but is rarely applied to LLM evaluation, and---where it is---the
items are typically repurposed human exams rather than instruments built
for, and calibrated on, machine examinees. In a specialized regulatory
domain there is no large pool of human test-takers to calibrate against,
and crowd-sourced items lack the domain subtlety that makes compliance
hard.

% Contributions.
This paper builds a difficulty-calibrated compliance item bank
\emph{end to end} and then uses it as a measuring instrument. Our
contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Automated item generation with a difficulty mandate.}
  We generate compliance scenarios under an explicit ``Red Herring +
  subtle violation'' construction (Section~\ref{sec:itemgen}): every hard
  item must contain a compliant-but-suspicious element designed to
  mislead, and the true violation must be a procedural or data-integrity
  subtlety rather than an obvious lapse. A parallel track converts real
  FDA warning letters into items to ground the synthetic bank in actual
  enforcement.

  \item \textbf{IRT calibration with a synthetic examinee population.}
  Lacking human test-takers, we administer each item to a population of
  synthetic AI examinees and estimate difficulty from their response
  pattern (Sections~\ref{sec:calibration}--\ref{sec:pipeline}). We fit a
  Rasch (1PL) model, anchor it to a fixed baseline examinee, and apply
  classical item-fit quality control to freeze a healthy bank of
  \textbf{1{,}284} items.

  \item \textbf{$\theta$ as an optimization metric, with a
  format-vs-reasoning decomposition.} Treating the calibrated bank as a
  fixed instrument, we measure how much a system-prompt intervention
  moves a model's ability $\theta$ (Section~\ref{sec:deltatheta}). A
  strict/lenient dual-judge design separates the portion of the gain
  attributable to \emph{citation format} from the portion reflecting
  genuine \emph{compliance reasoning}.
\end{enumerate}

We frame the work in two parts. Part~1
(Sections~\ref{sec:itemgen}--\ref{sec:results}) is about the
\emph{instrument}: how the calibrated bank is built and what it looks
like. Part~2 (Section~\ref{sec:deltatheta}) is an \emph{experiment} run
on that instrument. We are deliberately explicit about the limitations of
a closed loop in which the same model family generates, answers, and
judges (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
