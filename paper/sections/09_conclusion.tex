\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented an end-to-end pipeline that builds and IRT-calibrates a
difficulty-graded question bank for biopharma regulatory compliance, and
then uses that bank as a measuring instrument. The instrument
(Part~1) generates hard items under a Red Herring + subtle-violation
mandate, administers them to a synthetic examinee population, and freezes
a healthy bank of 1{,}284 Rasch-calibrated items spanning roughly $\pm 4$
logits, with a real-FDA track confirming that enforced violations are
harder than synthetic ones. The experiment (Part~2) treats the calibrated
ability $\theta$ as an optimization metric and, via a strict/lenient
dual-judge decomposition, shows that system-prompt strictness primarily
unlocks citation \emph{format} rather than compliance \emph{reasoning}---
with only the largest model showing a genuine reasoning gain.

The natural next steps follow the limitations: replace the analytic
difficulty SE with \texttt{py-irt} posterior standard deviations, add
human expert validation of a stratified subset, formally test the
real-vs-synthetic difficulty gap, and expand the examinee population
across model families to reduce correlation. Beyond measurement, the
calibrated bank enables adaptive item selection (choosing the item whose
difficulty best matches a system's current $\theta$) and a principled way
to track whether agentic or retrieval interventions---not just prompt
edits---move regulatory \emph{reasoning} on a stable scale. The
$\Delta\theta$ decomposition in Part~2 constitutes the core empirical
result of this phase: system-prompt strictness primarily buys citation
format, not compliance reasoning, except for the largest model tested.
Phase~3b will extend this framework to architectural interventions---
knowledge retrieval, self-critique, and step decomposition---to test
whether the bank can detect ability gains from structural reasoning
support.
